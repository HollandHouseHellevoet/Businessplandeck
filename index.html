II. Where the Money Goes

Each check lands somewhere.
None of it lands with you.

Float retained by carriers. Surplus accumulating on carrier balance sheets. Commission paid to brokers. Network access fees collected by PPO networks. Adjudication fees paid to ASOs. Reinsurance margins captured upstream. Every one of those dollars came from a patient encounter at your facility. None of them stay.

01 / Float
Carrier float
Premium paid up front. Claims paid months later. The capital in between belongs to the carrier.
02 / Surplus
Carrier surplus
When claims come in lower than expected, the unused premium accrues to the carrier's balance sheet, not yours.
03 / Commission
Broker commission
Pulled off the top of premium every renewal. The broker is paid whether the program performs or not.
04 / Network
Network access
PPO lease fees and rental network margins. Paid for the right to send your patients to a list of names.
05 / Adjudication
ASO adjudication
Per-employee-per-month fees paid to an Administrative Services Only platform for processing claims you funded.

III. The Five Names You Have Been Paying for 20 Years

Carriers. Brokers. ASOs.
PPO networks. Reinsurers.

What each one does. What each one charges. Why each one renews automatically. You have been signing these checks since before the ACA passed. The structure has never been laid out in front of you. Here it is.

Carriers
What they do

Hold the policy. Bear the named risk on paper. Adjudicate claims, set reserves, price renewals using the data you generated.

What they collect

8% to 18%

of premium as margin. Plus the float, plus the surplus, plus the renewal upside.

Why it renews

They own the claims data. You see a rate, not the underlying math. The renewal arrives as a number, not a negotiation.

Brokers
What they do

Place the program with a carrier. Send the renewal paperwork. Show up at the annual meeting. Sometimes call the carrier on your behalf.

What they collect

3% to 8%

commission off the top of premium. Some carry a contingent override on retention or growth.

Why it renews

They are the only person you have a phone number for. Replacing a broker means rebuilding every certificate and policy file under a new agency. Most operators do not have time for that.

ASOs
What they do

Process claims for a self-funded plan. Aetna, UMR, Meritain, others. Pure transaction processing, no risk-bearing.

What they collect

$30 to $65 PEPM

per employee per month, plus claim-handling fees and access charges baked into the contract.

Why it renews

Migrating an ASO mid-plan-year is operational chaos: new ID cards, network re-mapping, run-out claims. The default is to keep paying.

PPO Networks
What they do

Lease access to a list of contracted physicians and facilities at negotiated rates. Resold by carriers and ASOs to anyone who needs a network.

What they collect

$8 to $25 PEPM

network access fee. Often a percentage of savings on top, calculated against an opaque billed-charge benchmark.

Why it renews

Without the network lease, members get balance-billed at out-of-network rates. The fee is the price of a list of names that already exists in public directories.

Reinsurers
What they do

Assume the layer of risk above a retention threshold. Stop-loss above $250K per claim, catastrophic property above $5M, malpractice excess above policy limits.

What they collect

15% to 35%

margin on ceded premium. Reinsurance is the most profitable line in insurance because it is the least visible.

Why it renews

A bad claim year resets the market. Without an existing reinsurance relationship, getting placed mid-cycle requires a broker, a placement, and a meaningful rate increase. Continuity wins.

Five players. None of them write a check to you. All of them collect a check from you. Every year, automatically, on a renewal cycle the structure was built to protect.

X. Killing the Renewal Cycle

The problem with healthcare benefits
is that the renewals occur annually.

Every twelve months the price resets and the party with the most information sets the new number. A forward contract eliminates that leverage by locking the price for two or three years. The employer buys utilization in advance at a known rate. The physician gets revenue certainty. Neither needs the carrier to set the terms.

Annual renewal

The renewal clock

  • The whole plan resets every twelve months. Carriers own the claims data, the actuarial model, and the deadline.
  • Employers negotiate blind. Physicians are not at the table.
  • Year-over-year volatility compounds. Budget certainty is impossible.
  • Every renewal is a chance to re-price the information gap.
Forward contract

The physician's lock

  • Price fixed for two to three years. Both sides agree to the rate and the volume band in advance.
  • The employer gets budget certainty. The physician gets revenue certainty.
  • No annual renegotiation. No information asymmetry at renewal.
  • The carrier is not between the two parties that actually do the work.

The emergency room example.

Emergency room visits are the single most volatile line item in any self-funded health plan. They cannot be scheduled. They cannot be steered at the point of need. They are the cost category that scares every CFO looking at next year's budget.

A self-funded employer with 5,000 covered lives can model approximately 850 ER visits per year using demographic data. A forward contract with a physician-owned micro hospital locks the per-visit price for two to three years. The employer gets cost certainty on the line item that scares them most. The micro hospital ER, which is most of this audience, gets guaranteed volume at a known rate. The carrier loses leverage on the most volatile cost category.

Captives capture premium. Direct contracts capture pricing. Forward contracts kill the renewal cycle. The exchange institutionalizes price discovery across the entire market. Three legislative priorities.

I Priority I

Repeal Section 6001

The physician-owned hospital moratorium. Banned new construction in 2010 under the ACA. 187 facilities remain, frozen in place. Repeal opens the market for physician-led facility investment.

II Priority II

Reform CON Laws

Certificate of Need statutes in 34 states plus Washington D.C. let incumbent systems block new competitors from building. The laws were written in 1974. The incumbents they protect were small then. They are monopolies now.

III Priority III

Nonprofit accountability

Nonprofit hospital systems carry tax-exempt balance sheets, pay executives eight-figure compensation, sue patients for unpaid bills, and report community benefit as a percentage of revenue. Enforce the standard or remove the exemption.
