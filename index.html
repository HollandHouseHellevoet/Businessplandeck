II. The Architecture of Dependence

Independence is not failing.
It is being priced out.

Physicians practice medicine as well as they ever have. What has changed is the economic terrain around them. Pricing, facility access, and the right to build have each been captured by a small set of incumbents. The independent physician is not losing a fair fight. She is paying the rent on the ring.

III. The Missing Layer

The gap is not clinical.
It is financial.

Large hospital systems do not win on clinical quality. They win on four economic advantages that have nothing to do with medicine. Independent physicians have none of them. That is the gap. It is the only gap that matters.

Compliance, HR, revenue cycle, contracting, legal. One team serving one practice is a cost center. One team serving two hundred is infrastructure.

What independence needs.

The independent physician does not need a better EHR. She does not need a consultant. She needs the economic infrastructure of a large system without surrendering ownership or governance. A way to pool risk, capture float, negotiate as one, and administer at scale, while remaining a separate business that answers to its own physicians.

That is a single, specific piece of infrastructure. It has not existed at scale. Until now it has been easier to sell the practice than to build it.

What does that infrastructure look like?

IX. Forward Contracts

Lock the price.
Kill the renewal cycle.

The problem with direct contracts: they renew annually. Every twelve months, the price resets, and the party with the most information sets the new number. A forward contract eliminates that leverage by locking the price for two or three years. The employer buys utilization in advance at a known rate. The physician gets revenue certainty. Neither needs the carrier to set the terms.

Annual renewal

The carrier’s clock

  • Price resets every twelve months. The carrier owns the claims data, the actuarial model, and the renewal deadline.
  • The employer negotiates blind. The physician is not at the table.
  • Year-over-year volatility compounds. Budget certainty is impossible.
  • Every renewal is a chance for the carrier to re-price the information gap.
Forward contract

The physician’s lock

  • Price fixed for two to three years. Both sides agree to the rate and the volume band in advance.
  • The employer gets budget certainty. The physician gets revenue certainty.
  • No annual renegotiation. No information asymmetry at renewal.
  • The carrier is not between the two parties that actually do the work.

The math on one employer.

An employer with 5,000 covered lives knows, from demographic data, that it will need approximately 200 knee replacements over the next three years. Today that employer buys those procedures one at a time, at a rate it discovers only after the claim is filed. Under a forward contract, the employer buys a volume band at a fixed price. The physician-owned facility gets guaranteed volume. The employer gets price certainty. The contract is a commodity forward. It is how every other market on earth works.

X. The Exchange

The world spends $15 trillion
on healthcare every year.
There is no exchange.

Not a single country on earth has a transparent, competitive marketplace where medical treatments are priced against quality. Every other commodity has one. Oil has NYMEX. Grain has the CBOT. Metals have the LME. Healthcare has negotiation behind closed doors, and the party with the most information sets the price. That is not complexity. That is a business model.

$15T
Global healthcare spend / year
The largest market in the world without exchange-based pricing. Every dollar priced in private negotiation between parties with asymmetric information.
0
Exchanges, globally
No futures, no options, no standardized contracts, no public price discovery. The absence is the product.

What an exchange does.

An exchange forces institutional buyers to compete on both price and quality simultaneously. Insurance does not do this. Regulation does not do this. Negotiation between two private parties does not do this. Only an exchange, with standardized contracts and transparent pricing, does this.

Every industry that adopted exchange-based pricing got cheaper, faster, and more transparent. Airlines after deregulation. Electricity after restructuring. Telecommunications after the breakup. Financial instruments after the creation of options exchanges. Every single one.

Governments, Medicare, Medicaid, commercial health plans, and self-funded employers can all participate. The exchange is agnostic to the payer. It cares about the contract.

The progression.

You cannot build an exchange without standardized contracts. You cannot write standardized contracts without forward pricing. You cannot offer forward pricing without direct contracting. You cannot do direct contracting at scale without captive infrastructure. MedMerge is building the stack from the bottom up. The exchange is the endgame. Every layer beneath it is live or in development.

The captive infrastructure already operates. Direct contracting is being written. Forward pricing is the thesis Dutch built his career on. The exchange is the destination. It will not happen next quarter. But every contract MedMerge writes now is a standardized instrument that moves the market one step closer to the only thing it has never had: a price the buyer and the seller both know before the work begins.
