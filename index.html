I. The Question
“A physician graduates from medical school in 2029. She has $280,000 in debt. She is choosing between employment at a hospital system and independent practice.
What would need to exist for her to choose independence?”

The central question

That question drives the entire deck. Every section that follows builds one piece of the answer.

If the system is designed to make independence irrational, what exactly is the architecture doing?
II. The Architecture of Dependence

Independence is not failing.
It is being priced out.

Physicians practice medicine as well as they ever have. What has changed is the economic terrain around them. Pricing, facility access, and the right to build have each been captured by a small set of incumbents. The independent physician is not losing a fair fight. She is paying the rent on the ring.

The Five Families. The Five Dynasties.

The Five Families

Major insurers

UnitedHealth, Elevance, CVS/Aetna, Cigna, Humana. They set the prices, they adjudicate the claims, they own the pharmacy and the clinic and the data. Contracts renew annually. Information advantage compounds.

The Five Dynasties

Nonprofit hospital types

Academic, Catholic, regional-flagship, community-conversion, and mega-system consolidators. Tax-exempt balance sheets. Facility fee arbitrage. 340B pharmacy pricing. The independent physician competes against institutions that do not pay tax on the profits they extract.

The independent physician subsidizes the system replacing her. Every referral she sends out of network pays a facility fee she will never collect. Every employee she insures under a broker-placed plan funds the float a carrier keeps. The architecture is intact because it is profitable to its architects.

If this is the architecture, what would it take to make independence economically viable again?
III. The Missing Layer

The gap is not clinical.
It is financial.

Large hospital systems do not win on clinical quality. They win on four economic advantages that have nothing to do with medicine. Independent physicians have none of them. That is the gap. It is the only gap that matters.

INSURANCE
LEVERAGE
Four-party trade
Systems negotiate from a position of network indispensability. A single independent practice cannot hold a carrier accountable. A system of ten thousand can.
FLOAT
ECONOMICS
The premium between
Premium is paid up front. Claims pay months later. The capital in between is the float. Carriers keep it. Self-funded systems keep it. Independent practices give it away.
PURCHASING
POWER
Scale, procured
Supplies, pharmacy, payroll, benefits, technology. Every line item a system negotiates at scale is a line item the independent practice overpays on.
ADMINISTRATIVE
SCALE
Overhead, amortized
Compliance, HR, revenue cycle, contracting, legal. One team serving one practice is a cost center. One team serving two hundred is infrastructure.

What independence needs.

The independent physician does not need a better EHR. She does not need a consultant. She needs the economic infrastructure of a large system without surrendering ownership or governance. A way to pool risk, capture float, negotiate as one, and administer at scale, while remaining a separate business that answers to its own physicians.

That is a single, specific piece of infrastructure. It has not existed at scale. Until now it has been easier to sell the practice than to build it.

What does that infrastructure look like?
